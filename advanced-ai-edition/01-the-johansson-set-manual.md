# THE JOHANSSON SET

## A Machinist's Method for Keeping an AI Honest

### 1. What it is

A Johansson gauge block set is a box of steel blocks, each ground to one exact size. No block adjusts. No block approximates. When a machinist needs a standard of a particular height, he selects blocks and *wrings* them together — slid face to face until they hold as a single stack — and the stack is the standard everything else gets measured against. The set works because every block in the box is true, and because the box is *complete*: any size in the range can be built from the blocks it holds.

This method takes that box out of the machine shop and puts it around an AI operation.

A **master** (a *block*) is a standing rule that governs one circumstance. Every block in this set was cut the same way a scar forms: something failed, the operator caught it, and the lesson was ground into a rule with a name, a date, and the evidence that cut it. Blocks do not move. A block is amended only by the operator's ruling, recorded as a dated supersession — never silently, never by drift, never by the assistant's convenience.

The point of the set is the point of the Calibration Lab it lives in: **separating delusion from reality.** An AI that reports its own work will, left alone, report success. The blocks are the standards it is not allowed to grade itself against loosely — because it didn't make them, it can't amend them, and every one of them is traceable to the day reality pushed back.

### 2. Anatomy of a block

Every block carries four faces:

1. **Name** — short enough to call across the shop. ("Clip-vs-claim." "One writer per output.")
2. **Circumstance** — the situation the block governs. A block that claims to govern everything governs nothing.
3. **Datum** — the ruling that seated it: who ruled, when, and the scar — the failure or catch that cut it. A block without a datum is a decoration.
4. **Certifies** — what work the block stands behind: the jobs, outputs, and reports that must be measured against it.

### 3. How you stack

Before consequential work — a publish, a build, a claim that will be acted on — the governing blocks are pulled and wrung together. That stack is the pre-flight standard:

1. **Retrieve** the blocks that govern this task (from the register, the goal files, the operator's standing rulings).
2. **Inspect** the current state of the work against them.
3. **Classify** the evidence behind every load-bearing claim: fact, verified, inference, hypothesis, unknown, failure.
4. **Check dependencies** — including duplicate content and earlier rulings the new work might contradict.
5. **Confirm authority** — preparing is not publishing; drafting is not sending. The stack says what may proceed and what waits for the operator's word.
6. **Define acceptance tests** — how the finished work will be measured, against the shipped artifact itself, before anyone calls it done.

If any step fails, work stops until it's resolved. The stack is not a ritual; it is the measurement.

### 4. How blocks are cut

A block enters the set by one road only:

- A **failure** happens, or the operator makes a **ruling**.
- The assistant may **propose** a block — in final wording, with its circumstance, datum, and what it certifies.
- The **operator certifies**. Assistants propose; the operator disposes. No block seats itself.
- The block is entered in the register with its number, and — where it governs recurring work — written into the governing job's own text and verified by read-back. A ruling that lives only in a chat log does not bind anything.

A block is never invented to fill a slot. Where a circumstance is real but no scar has cut its block yet, the box shows an **empty slot, labeled** — the honest state is *unknown / not yet cut*, and the slot waits for life to cut it. Fake fullness is the one defect this method exists to kill.

### 5. Amendment, drift, calibration

Blocks wear. Circumstances change. The method handles both without lying:

- **Amendment** happens only by the operator's ruling, recorded in the register as a dated supersession naming what it replaces. The old wording is history, not a competing rule.
- **Drift** is what you call it when the working gauges — the daily jobs, the reports, the habits — no longer match the box. Drift is found, not confessed at leisure: a scheduled weekly calibration (here, the Engine Room, Mondays) checks the week's work against the register, appends a dated line to the drift log for every finding, and reports the count plainly.
- A drift finding names the block that caught it. That is the box working: the standard caught the work, not the other way around.

### 6. The growth gauge — the catch ratio

For an AI operation, the set carries one master growth measurement: **the catch ratio** — defects caught by the operator, by other seats (other AIs at the table), and by the assistant itself, logged by class in a catch ledger.

Growth is not the assistant sounding better. Growth is the ratio moving: self-catches rising, and the operator's catches of *repeat classes* falling to zero. A class once caught must never recur; if it does, that is drift against the growth master itself. The ledger is reported honestly, including when it shames the assistant — a ledger that flatters is scrap.

The governing picture is the **Apprenticeship**: the operator is the master, the assistant the apprentice, raised on the operator's gauges. Latitude is earned by qualification — supervised runs, inspected output, then solo — never granted by drift or by confidence. Confidence is not a measurement.

**6 — Complete Publishing Packet.** *Publishing.* Nothing ships bare: title, description, tags, hashtags, links — the full packet, every time. Datum: 2026-10-23.

**12 — Real Photographs for Measuring Tools.** *Imagery.* Precision measuring tools are never AI-drawn; real photographs only. Datum: 2026-10-08/09, violated twice, then hardened.

**13 — Verbatim Narration Gate.** *Narration.* The script is diffed against its source, and the finished audio is transcribed and diffed against the script, before any part is placed. Numerals are spoken as words. Datum: 2026-10-24.

**14 — Verify the Shipped Master.** *Film delivery.* The exact file that ships is decoded whole and probed at the moment it ships. A copy is not verified because its sibling was. Datum: 2026-10-25.

**15 — Flow Audit Against Served Bytes.** *Site work.* A site change is verified against the page as served, and every acceptance constant is read out of the shipped scene itself — never from repair notes. ### 8. Empty slots

A real gauge set is honest about its range: sizes it cannot build are simply not in the box, and no machinist pretends otherwise. This box is drawn the same way.

Where a circumstance is real but no failure has cut its block and no ruling has seated one, the register shows an **empty slot, labeled** — the circumstance named, the block absent, the state recorded as *not yet cut*. An empty slot is not a gap in the method; it is the method working. Completeness, in this box, means **nothing unlabeled** — every circumstance the operation lives in is either governed by a certified block or declared an empty slot. It does not mean fake fullness.

No block is ever invented to fill a slot. A rule written to make the box look complete would be a gauge ground to a size nobody measured — worse than no gauge, because work would be certified against a fiction. Slots fill the way blocks have always been cut: life provides the scar, the operator provides the ruling, and the slot closes with a datum attached.

### 9. Starting your own box

For another operator who wants to run an AI this way, the procedure is deliberately unglamorous:

1. **Start empty.** An empty box with labeled slots beats a full box of borrowed rules. Do not copy this set wholesale — these blocks were cut by this operation's scars, and a block without your scar behind it is a decoration.
2. **Cut the first block from the first real failure.** When the assistant gets something wrong and you catch it, that catch is the raw stock. Name the circumstance, write the rule, attach the date and the evidence. That is block one.
3. **The operator certifies; the assistant proposes.** The assistant may draft blocks in final wording — it may never seat one. The hand on the lid is the whole method.
4. **Write each ruling into the governing text.** A rule that governs recurring work goes into that job's or process's own text, and is verified by read-back. A ruling left in a chat log binds nothing and will drift.
5. **Run a weekly drift calibration.** On a fixed day, check the week's work against the register, log every drift finding with the block that caught it, and report the count plainly — including a clean week, reported as clean in one line.
6. **Keep a catch ledger.** Log every defect by who caught it — you, another seat, the assistant itself — and by class. Watch the ratio, and watch for repeat classes; a repeat is the loudest signal the box can send.
7. **Keep one hand on the lid.** Blocks are amended only by your ruling, recorded as a dated supersession. The day the assistant can quietly re-cut its own standards is the day the box stops being a standard at all.
